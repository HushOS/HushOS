<Step id="password" n={1} title={contents.password}>

When you sign in, your device proves it knows your password without sending it, not even in a scrambled form. For your password, we keep only a record that can check that proof. Guessing your password from that record means trying passwords one at a time, and each try is slowed down on purpose. Signing in with an email that has no account gets the same kind of answer, so the sign-in page doesn’t reveal who has one.

Once you’re in, your browser holds a session that ends after 7 days unused, or after 30 days at most.

<Exact open>

<Terms
    rows={[
        ['Protocol', 'OPAQUE (Ristretto), Serenity implementation'],
        ['Hardening', 'Argon2id, 65,536 KiB memory, 3 iterations, parallelism 4'],
        ['Server stores', 'The OPAQUE registration record'],
        ['Client receives', 'An export key that only the client ever sees'],
        ['Unknown email', 'Answered with a decoy record so lookups do not reveal accounts'],
    ]}
/>

OPAQUE produces two secrets. The **session key** is shared with the server; HushOS uses a separate random token for its application sessions. The **export key** is known only to the client. Only the export key is used to wrap the account key, and only after a further derivation step.

<Terms
    title="Sessions and transport"
    rows={[
        [
            'Verification links',
            '32 random bytes, sent in a URL fragment, stored only as SHA-256, valid 30 minutes',
        ],
        ['Login attempts', 'Random handles, stored hashed, consumed after 5 minutes or one finish'],
        ['Sessions', '7 days idle, 30 days at most, 32-byte token, only its SHA-256 stored'],

        ['Mutations', 'Exact Origin check, JSON only, 16 KiB body limit, per-address rate limits'],
        ['Responses', 'private, no-store and no-referrer'],
    ]}
/>

</Exact>

</Step>

<Step id="keys" n={2} title={contents.keys}>

When you sign up, your device makes the key for your account. Your password locks one copy of it and your recovery phrase locks another. Changing your password locks the same key again, so nothing else has to change.

Your device also makes your identity keys, which are what other people lock things to when they share with you, and a key for your workspace. Every folder and file hangs off the workspace key, each with a key of its own.

<Exact>

Your account key, also called the master key, is 32 random bytes generated in your browser. It is not derived from your password. Two independent wrappings of the same key exist:

<Terms
    rows={[
        ['Password wrap', 'HKDF-SHA-256 over the OPAQUE export key, then XChaCha20-Poly1305'],
        ['Recovery wrap', 'HKDF-SHA-256 over the recovery secret, then XChaCha20-Poly1305'],
        ['Salt and nonce', 'Fresh 32-byte salt and 24-byte nonce on every wrap'],
        ['Bound to', 'Your account ID, root revision, and credential or recovery revision'],
    ]}
/>

Every envelope carries associated data that names its purpose, version, and owner. An envelope moved to another account or another version fails to open.

At signup the browser also generates an X25519 key pair for encryption, an Ed25519 key pair for signatures, and an ML-KEM-768 key pair, the post-quantum key exchange NIST standardised as FIPS 203. The private halves are wrapped with keys derived from the account key and bound to their own public keys, so a swapped public key is detected when the envelope is opened. Only public keys and wrapped private material reach the server. The X25519 and ML-KEM keys together are what a share is sealed to, and what a report’s keys are escrowed to.

<Terms
    title="Identity keys"
    rows={[
        ['Encryption', 'X25519, libsodium'],
        [
            'Post-quantum',
            'ML-KEM-768 (FIPS 203), expanded from a 64-byte seed wrapped under the account key',
        ],
        ['Signatures', 'Ed25519, from a 32-byte seed wrapped under the account key'],
        [
            'Binding',
            'The Ed25519 key signs the ML-KEM key together with the X25519 key and your account ID',
        ],
    ]}
/>

The server refuses to store an ML-KEM public key without that signature. It is not folded into the fingerprint, so the fingerprint two people compare stays the X25519 one.

Every workspace has its own 32-byte key, generated in your browser. It is random, not derived from your account key, so it can be granted to another person later without giving them anything of yours. What you hold is a **grant**: the workspace key wrapped under a key derived from your account key.

<Terms
    title="Workspace key"
    rows={[
        ['Workspace key', '32 random bytes, created on your device when the workspace is'],
        ['Grant wrap', 'HKDF-SHA-256 over the account key, then XChaCha20-Poly1305'],
        [
            'Bound to',
            'Your account ID, the workspace ID, your root revision, and the workspace key revision',
        ],
        ['Server stores', 'One grant per member per workspace, never the key'],
    ]}
/>

Folder and file keys hang off the workspace key, not off your account key. That is what keeps rotation cheap: replacing your account key rewraps one grant per workspace you belong to, and nothing beneath it moves.

<Terms
    title="In your browser"
    rows={[
        ['Account key', 'Held in plaintext only inside a Web Worker, never in application state'],
        [
            'Remembered unlock',
            'The worker wraps the account key with a non-exportable AES-256-GCM device key held in IndexedDB; locking or signing out clears it across tabs in that browser',
        ],
        [
            'Tree mirror',
            'Your tree as the server holds it, ciphertext only, kept in IndexedDB so search and folder listings run on the device; cleared when you leave the workspace',
        ],
    ]}
/>

How remembered unlock and the account key rotations work, step by step, is in the [accounts and keys design document](https://github.com/HushOS/HushOS/blob/main/docs/opaque-auth-design.md).

</Exact>

</Step>

<Step id="recovery" n={3} title={contents.recovery}>

The 24 words you save at sign-up lock their own copy of your account key. With the phrase and a link we email you, you can set a new password and your files stay as they were. Email alone can never get you back in. Resetting your password this way gives you a new phrase and signs you out everywhere else.

In [Account](/app/account) you can also change your password, make a new recovery phrase, or reset your sharing keys (a new account key). Each one asks for your current password and signs you out of your other browsers.

<Exact>

<Terms
    rows={[
        ['Phrase', 'A 32-byte secret made on your device, shown as 24 English BIP39 words'],
        [
            'Backup',
            'The secret is also stored encrypted by the account key, so the phrase can be shown again while your device is unlocked',
        ],
        ['Reset needs', 'A verified email link and a signature from a key derived from the phrase'],
        [
            'After a reset',
            'The unchanged account key is rewrapped, every session is revoked, a new phrase is issued, and you are signed in with the new password',
        ],
    ]}
/>

During a reset the browser re-registers with OPAQUE under the new password and rewraps the unchanged account key. The server checks the signature and every binding and replaces the credentials in one transaction.

<Terms
    title="In Account"
    rows={[
        [
            'Change password',
            'Rewraps the same master key; preserves the recovery phrase and identity keys',
        ],
        [
            'Make a new phrase',
            'Creates new 24-word recovery credentials; preserves the password, master key, and identity keys',
        ],
        [
            'Reset sharing keys',
            'Generates a new master key and recovery phrase; rewraps the existing identity keys and every workspace grant, and preserves the password',
        ],
    ]}
/>

Each action needs your current password through a fresh OPAQUE exchange, revokes existing sessions and pending recovery attempts, and rejects stale concurrent changes. After either rotation, save and confirm the new recovery phrase before continuing into the app. The full sequence is in the [accounts and keys design document](https://github.com/HushOS/HushOS/blob/main/docs/opaque-auth-design.md).

</Exact>

</Step>

<Step id="files" n={4} title={contents.files}>

Your device locks each file, and its name, type and modified time, before any of it leaves. From a locked copy itself, our servers learn only roughly how large the file is. The locked pieces go straight from your device to storage; our app server never handles them. Thumbnails are made on your device and locked inside the file itself. Downloads come back the same way and are unlocked on your device, and previews fetch only the parts they need.

<Exact>

Every folder and file in Drive has its own key, wrapped under its parent’s key; the root folder’s key is wrapped under the workspace key. A file’s key locks its details and wraps a separate content key for each version of the file. The server holds a tree of ids and envelopes.

<Terms
    title="Folder and file keys"
    rows={[
        ['Node key', '32 random bytes per folder or file, wrapped under the parent node key'],
        [
            'Metadata',
            'Name, type, size and modified time, encrypted under the node key and padded to 256-byte steps, so its length does not follow the name',
        ],
        ['Content key', '32 random bytes per file version, wrapped under the node key'],
        ['Wrap', 'XChaCha20-Poly1305 with a fresh 24-byte nonce'],
        [
            'Bound to',
            'The workspace, the node, its parent and the parent key epoch, or the version and object',
        ],
    ]}
/>

An envelope moved to another folder, another version or another workspace fails to open. A key epoch counts each time a node’s key changes; it is what lets a rotation tell old envelopes from new ones.

<Terms
    title="Uploads and downloads"
    rows={[
        ['Cipher', 'XChaCha20-Poly1305, one 16-byte tag per 8 MiB chunk'],
        ['Chunk nonces', 'Derived from a 16-byte per-object nonce and the chunk index'],
        [
            'Transfer',
            'Browser to object store directly, by signed URLs valid 15 minutes for shared content, an hour otherwise',
        ],
        ['Resume', 'A journal on your device records each chunk’s digest before it is sent'],
        [
            'Thumbnails',
            'Rendered on the uploader’s device and sealed into the file’s own encrypted object, under a key derived from the content key; the server cannot tell which files have one',
        ],
        [
            'Size',
            'The server records the size the store confirms for the encrypted object; the file’s own size is sealed in its envelope',
        ],
    ]}
/>

Independent chunks are what make ranged previews and resumed uploads plain arithmetic. A chunk is never re-encrypted under the same key and nonce with different bytes: a resumed upload proves the file is the same one, chunk digest by chunk digest, or starts over with fresh material.

</Exact>

</Step>

<Step id="sharing" n={5} title={contents.sharing}>

Sharing a folder or file with another HushOS account locks a copy of its key for that one person, on your device, so only their devices can open it. The lock uses two methods together, one of them designed to resist future quantum computers, so a copy of our database taken today stays shut even if the older method is broken later.

We hand out the keys people share to, so HushOS lets you check them. The first time you share with someone, their key is remembered, and you can both compare a short fingerprint. If their key ever changes, HushOS shows a warning and uses the new key only after you accept it.

<Exact>

The seal is hybrid: an X25519 agreement between your identity key and theirs, and an ML-KEM-768 encapsulation to their post-quantum key, combined into one key that seals the node key. Opening it needs both private halves. The recipient mounts the shared item as a root of its own under “Shared with me”.

<Terms
    rows={[
        [
            'Seal',
            'X25519 agreement plus ML-KEM-768 encapsulation, HKDF-SHA-256 over both secrets, then XChaCha20-Poly1305 over the node key',
        ],
        ['Envelope', '1160 bytes: nonce, the 1088-byte ML-KEM ciphertext, and the sealed node key'],
        ['Whose key', 'A contact key you pinned, with a fingerprint you can compare in person'],
        ['Roles', 'Viewer, or editor; an editor’s uploads land in your storage'],
        [
            'Authorization',
            'Checked on every request by walking up the tree to the nearest live share',
        ],
        ['Server holds', 'The sealed box, the role, and who shared with whom'],
    ]}
/>

Because the server serves public keys, a dishonest server could substitute one of its own. That is why contacts are pinned: the first key you use is recorded in your encrypted settings, and a later change is shown as a warning that you must accept, never applied silently. The fingerprint covers the X25519 key; the post-quantum key is vouched for by the contact’s signing key, checked on your device before it is used, and its digest joins the pin. A different post-quantum key served later is refused like any changed key, and one that goes missing is refused as a downgrade. Every path that seals to a contact goes through the pin, including the re-keying that follows a revocation.

</Exact>

</Step>

<Step id="links" n={6} title={contents.links}>

The secret part of a link sits after the # in the address, which browsers never send to a server. If you add a password, it is needed too, and we can’t tell whether there is one. Links can end on a date up to a year away, and you can turn one off at any time. Anyone with the link opens it in their own browser, without an account.

<Exact>

A link is a share without a recipient. The node key is sealed under a key derived from the link’s secret and, if you set one, a password stretched with Argon2id on the visitor’s device. The server holds the sealed box and a hash of the link’s path; it can open nothing.

<Terms
    rows={[
        ['Secret', '32 random bytes in the URL fragment, never sent to the server'],
        ['Password', 'Argon2id (64 MiB, 3 iterations) with a per-link salt, mixed into the key'],
        ['Expiry', 'Optional, at most a year; revocation is immediate'],
        ['Visitor', 'Browses, previews and downloads in their own browser, with no account'],
        ['Keeping a copy', 'A signed-in visitor re-encrypts each file under their own keys'],
    ]}
/>

</Exact>

</Step>

<Step id="stop" n={7} title={contents.stop}>

When you stop a share or turn off a link, that person is refused on their next request. Then your device changes the keys for that folder and everything in it, and locks them again for everyone who still has access. Nothing the removed person kept opens anything new.

<Exact>

<Terms
    rows={[
        ['Refused', 'On the next request, on every route'],
        [
            'Keys',
            'The shared item and everything beneath it get fresh keys under a new epoch; the old keys are gone',
        ],
        [
            'Re-sealed',
            'Every remaining share and link, under the new keys, to the pinned keys of each remaining person',
        ],
        ['Content', 'Not re-encrypted; the guarantee is about keys and authorization'],
        ['Download links', 'Already issued ones expire within 15 minutes'],
    ]}
/>

How a rotation visits every item, survives moves made while it runs, and keeps older envelopes apart is in the [Drive design document](https://github.com/HushOS/HushOS/blob/main/docs/drive-design.md).

</Exact>

</Step>

<Step id="reports" n={8} title={contents.reports}>

Anyone who can see something through a share or a link can report it. Reporting is the only way the people who run the service can see a file: the reporter’s device locks that one item’s key to them. They can then dismiss the report, remove the content, hold it, suspend the uploader, or pass it to the relevant authority. Nothing you haven’t shared can be reported, because nobody else can see it.

<Exact>

An operator opens a report under their own identity.

</Exact>

</Step>

<Section id="where" title={contents.where}>

HushOS is open source, and anyone can run their own copy. Who runs the copy you use decides where it runs.

<Terms
    rows={[
        [
            'hushos.com',
            'Run by HushOS, a company incorporated in Delaware, United States, with its servers and storage in the European Union',
        ],
        [
            'A self-hosted copy',
            'Run by whoever set it up, on their own servers, under their own terms',
        ],
    ]}
/>

Either way, your browser sends locked files straight to the object store, and the app server never handles file bytes. Every page is served with a content security policy that limits which scripts run and where the page can connect, and with the usual protective headers.

<Exact>

Every page carries a Content Security Policy: scripts run only from the app’s own origin, under a per-request nonce, with WebAssembly allowed and `eval` not. The page may connect only to itself and to the object store. Every response carries `X-Content-Type-Options`, `X-Frame-Options: DENY`, a referrer policy, a cross-origin opener policy, a permissions policy that switches off camera, microphone, location and payment, and, over HTTPS, HSTS for a year.

</Exact>

<Section id="limits" title={contents.limits}>

- If you lose your password, your recovery phrase and every browser that remembers you, your files are gone. Nobody can get them back, including us.
- Someone who downloaded a file while they had access keeps that copy. Stopping a share protects what comes next; it can’t reach into someone else’s device.
- Signing out other browsers takes effect when they next check in with the server. It can’t erase keys a device already holds offline.
- Replacing your recovery phrase stops the old one from resetting your password. A recovery kit you downloaded earlier still holds the old phrase and your account key as it was then, so it can still open that key offline. Password resets and a new recovery phrase keep the same account key; only replacing the account key makes a different one.
- Replacing your account key can’t erase old locked copies, or take back private identity keys someone already pulled out of an older key bundle.
- The server sees what’s listed above: your email, sizes, times and how your folders nest.
- A share made before the other person had a post-quantum key uses X25519 alone until your device locks it again with both. A report to an operator without a post-quantum key uses X25519 alone.

</Section>
