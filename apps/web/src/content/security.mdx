<Step id="password" n={1} title={contents.password}>

When you sign in, your device proves it knows your password without sending it, not even in a scrambled form. For your password, we keep only a record that can check that proof. Guessing your password from that record means trying passwords one at a time, and each try is slowed down on purpose. Signing in with an email that has no account gets the same kind of answer, so the sign-in page doesn’t reveal who has one.

Once you’re in, your browser holds a session that ends after 7 days unused, or after 30 days at most.

Each session belongs to a device. Your devices, in Account, lists every browser and phone signed in to your account. Removing one asks for your password and signs it out at once. The next time that device checks in, it learns it was removed and clears what it kept for your account; we can’t promise that, because a device kept offline never checks in. When a device we haven’t seen before signs in with your password, we email you.

<Step id="keys" n={2} title={contents.keys}>

When you sign up, your device makes the key for your account. Your password locks one copy of it and your recovery phrase locks another. Changing your password locks the same key again, so nothing else has to change.

Your device also makes your identity keys, which are what other people lock things to when they share with you, and a key for your workspace. Every folder and file hangs off the workspace key, each with a key of its own.

<Exact>

Your account key, also called the master key, is 32 random bytes generated in your browser. It is not derived from your password. Two independent wrappings of the same key exist:

<Terms
    rows={[
        ['Password wrap', 'HKDF-SHA-256 over the OPAQUE export key, then XChaCha20-Poly1305'],
        ['Recovery wrap', 'HKDF-SHA-256 over the recovery secret, then XChaCha20-Poly1305'],
        ['Salt and nonce', 'Fresh 32-byte salt and 24-byte nonce on every wrap'],
        ['Bound to', 'Your account ID, root revision, and credential or recovery revision'],
    ]}
/>

Every envelope carries associated data that names its purpose, version, and owner. An envelope moved to another account or another version fails to open.

At signup the browser also generates your identity: an X25519 key pair for encryption, an ML-KEM-768 key pair (the post-quantum key exchange NIST standardised as FIPS 203), and two signing key pairs, Ed25519 and ML-DSA-65 (the post-quantum signature standardised as FIPS 204). The private halves are wrapped with keys derived from the account key. Your identity signs a statement over all four public keys and your account ID with both signing keys; the server refuses an identity whose statement does not verify, and every device checks it again. Only public keys and wrapped private material reach the server. The X25519 and ML-KEM keys together are what a share is sealed to, and what a report’s key is sealed to.

The twelve words two people compare are version 3: a digest of the X25519 and Ed25519 keys and of the ML-DSA and ML-KEM keys, so comparing words checks all four.

Every workspace has its own 32-byte key, generated in your browser. It is random, not derived from your account key, so it can be granted to another person later without giving them anything of yours. What you hold is a **grant**: the workspace key wrapped under a key derived from your account key.

<Step id="recovery" n={3} title={contents.recovery}>

The 24 words you save at sign-up lock their own copy of your account key. With the phrase and a link we email you, you can set a new password and your files stay as they were. Email alone can never get you back in. Resetting your password this way gives you a new phrase and signs you out everywhere else.

In [Account](/app/account) you can also change your password, make a new recovery phrase, or rotate your account key. Each one asks for your current password and signs you out of your other browsers. You can also replace your sharing keys, which the people you share with follow without comparing words again (see sharing below).

<Step id="files" n={4} title={contents.files}>

Your device locks each file, and its name, type and modified time, before any of it leaves. Each file is padded before it is locked, so from a locked copy our servers learn only roughly how large the file is, to within a few percent. The locked pieces go straight from your device to storage; our app server never handles them. Thumbnails are made on your device and locked inside the file itself. Downloads come back the same way and are unlocked on your device, and previews fetch only the parts they need.

<PaddingDemo />

<Exact>

Every folder and file in Drive has its own key, wrapped under its parent’s key; the root folder’s key is wrapped under the workspace key. A file’s key locks its details and wraps a separate content key for each version of the file. The server holds a tree of ids and envelopes.

An envelope moved to another folder, another version or another workspace fails to open. A key epoch counts each time a node’s key changes; it is what lets a rotation tell old envelopes from new ones.

File contents are padded before they are encrypted. Below 256 bytes, everything is 256 bytes. Above that, at most 6.25% is added, so the server learns a file’s size only to within roughly 2 KiB around 50 KB, 32 KiB around 1 MB, 2 MiB around 100 MB and 32 MiB around 1 GB.

Every name and every version is also signed by the account that wrote it. Your device checks each signature against your own keys and the people whose words you compared, and marks anything it can’t tie to one of them as **Unverified**. It names the writer only from your own contacts: you, someone whose words you compared, or someone you haven’t checked; the server tells it nothing more than an account id. Keys someone has replaced no longer vouch for what they signed. If your device can’t run the check at all, because your contacts or your own earlier keys didn’t load, every item shows **Couldn’t check** until it can, never no mark. A key that only opens a folder, such as a link’s, can no longer plant a file or rename one unnoticed. Unverified items still open: it is a warning, not a lock.

<Step id="sharing" n={5} title={contents.sharing}>

Sharing a folder or file with another HushOS account locks a copy of its key for that one person, on your device, so only their devices can open it. The lock uses two methods together, one of them designed to resist future quantum computers, so a copy of our database taken today stays shut even if the older method is broken later.

Every share is locked with a fresh key made for it alone and signed by the person sharing, so a key stolen later doesn’t open what was shared before, and nobody else can pass a share off as theirs.

We hand out the keys people share to, so HushOS lets you check them. When you add someone, their keys are remembered after you both compare twelve words. If they replace their sharing keys in HushOS, the change is signed by their old keys: when you check them again, your device checks that signature, follows the change and tells you, so you can compare words again. It never follows on its own, so the server can’t learn whom you’ve added by watching what your device looks up. Any other change shows a warning, and the new key is used only after you accept it.

<Exact>

The seal is hybrid: an X25519 agreement between a fresh key made for this share and their identity key, and an ML-KEM-768 encapsulation to their post-quantum key, combined into one key that seals the node key. Opening it needs both private halves. Your identity signs the result with both its signing keys, Ed25519 and ML-DSA-65, and their device checks both against the keys it pinned for you before opening anything. The recipient mounts the shared item as a root of its own under “Shared with me”.

<Terms
    rows={[
        [

        ],
        [
            'Envelope',
            'Suite 4, 4565 bytes: the ephemeral key, the 1088-byte ML-KEM ciphertext, nonce, the sealed node key, and the sharer’s 3373-byte Ed25519 and ML-DSA-65 signature',
        ],
        ['Older suites', 'Suites 1 to 3 are refused: not accepted, not served, not opened'],
        [
            'Replacing keys',
            'A new identity at the next key version, signed with both keys of the one it replaces; a contact’s device asks for the whole chain and checks it from the keys it pinned, only when the person checks them again. Replaced keys vouch for nothing: what they signed shows as Unverified, and a share they signed waits to be shared again',
        ],
        [
            'Whose key',
            'A contact you pinned: version 3, holding their X25519 and Ed25519 keys and the digests of their ML-DSA and ML-KEM keys, with twelve words you can compare in person',
        ],
        ['Roles', 'Viewer, or editor; an editor’s uploads land in your storage'],
        [
            'Authorization',
            'Checked on every request by walking up the tree to the nearest live share',
        ],
        ['Server holds', 'The sealed box, the role, and who shared with whom'],
    ]}
/>

Because the server serves public keys, a dishonest server could substitute one of its own. That is why contacts are pinned: a person’s keys are recorded in your encrypted settings when you add them after comparing words, and a later change is shown as a warning that you must accept, never applied silently. Nothing is pinned without that check: something shared with you by a person you have not added stays shut until you add them. The words cover all four keys, and a served key that does not match the pin is shown as a changed key. A pin made before post-quantum identities trusts nothing until you compare words again. Shares are only ever sealed hybrid: a person without a post-quantum key yet is not shared with until they have one. Every path that seals to a contact goes through the pin, and the re-keying that follows a revocation seals only to the shares and links your own encrypted record says you made, whatever the server lists. Your device also remembers the newest version of your settings it has seen, and the newest version of your own sharing keys it has opened, and refuses an older copy of either, so the server cannot bring back a key you replaced. A new browser has nothing to compare against yet.

</Exact>

</Step>

<Step id="links" n={6} title={contents.links}>

The secret part of a link sits after the # in the address, which browsers never send to a server. If you add a password, it is needed too, and we can’t tell whether there is one. Links can end on a date up to a year away, and you can turn one off at any time. Anyone with the link opens it in their own browser, without an account.

<LinkAnatomy />

<Exact>

A link is a share without a recipient. The node key is sealed under a key derived from the link’s secret and, if you set one, a password stretched with Argon2id on the visitor’s device. The server holds the sealed box and a hash of the link’s path; it can open nothing.

<Step id="stop" n={7} title={contents.stop}>

When you stop a share or turn off a link, that person is refused on their next request. Then your device changes the keys for that folder and everything in it, and locks them again for everyone who still has access. Nothing the removed person kept opens anything new.

<Step id="reports" n={8} title={contents.reports}>

Anyone who can see something through a share or a link can report it. Reporting is the only way the people who run the service can see a file: the reporter’s device locks that one item’s key to them. They can then dismiss the report, remove the content, hold it, suspend the uploader, or pass it to the relevant authority. Nothing you haven’t shared can be reported, because nobody else can see it.

HushOS is open source, and anyone can run their own copy. Who runs the copy you use decides where it runs.

Either way, your browser sends locked files straight to the object store, and the app server never handles file bytes. Every page is served with a content security policy that limits which scripts run and where the page can connect, and with the usual protective headers.

<Exact>

Every page carries a Content Security Policy: scripts run only from the app’s own origin, under a per-request nonce, with WebAssembly allowed and `eval` not. The page may connect only to itself and to the object store. Every response carries `X-Content-Type-Options`, `X-Frame-Options: DENY`, a referrer policy, a cross-origin opener policy, a permissions policy that switches off camera, microphone, location and payment, and, over HTTPS, HSTS for a year.

<Section id="limits" title={contents.limits}>

- If you lose your password, your recovery phrase and every browser that remembers you, your files are gone. Nobody can get them back, including us.
- Someone who downloaded a file while they had access keeps that copy. Stopping a share protects what comes next; it can’t reach into someone else’s device.
- Signing out other browsers, or removing a device, takes effect when they next check in with the server. It can’t erase keys or files a device already holds offline.
- Replacing your recovery phrase stops the old one from resetting your password. A recovery kit you downloaded earlier still holds the old phrase and your account key as it was then, so it can still open that key offline. Password resets and a new recovery phrase keep the same account key; only replacing the account key makes a different one.
- Replacing your account key can’t erase old locked copies, or take back private identity keys someone already pulled out of an older key bundle.
- The server sees what’s listed above: your email, padded sizes, times, who wrote each change and how your folders nest. Padding hides a file’s exact size, not roughly how big it is.
- Shares made before post-quantum identities stay shut until you and the other person have compared words again and your device has sealed them anew.
- Items written before HushOS signed changes, and anything re-locked without a signature your device could verify, show as Unverified until someone writes them again.
- Whoever runs the server chooses which operator keys a report is sealed to, so they can open anything reported to them.
- Replacing sharing keys with stolen keys would look like a real replacement to the people you share with. That is why HushOS tells them, and suggests comparing words again.
- Signatures say who wrote an item, not that it is the newest version: the server could still serve an older, signed state of it. A report to an operator without a post-quantum key, and any report filed before reports became hybrid, is sealed under X25519 alone.

</Section>
